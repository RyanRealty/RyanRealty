## 1. Glennda Baker — Atlanta, GA

**Name:** Glennda Baker  
**Market:** Atlanta / Marietta, GA  
**Brokerage:** Ansley Atlanta Real Estate (associate broker)  

### Follower Trajectory

| Date | Platform | Followers |
|------|----------|-----------|
| Oct 2020 | TikTok | 122 |
| Jan 2021 (90 days) | TikTok | 100,000 |
| Late 2021 (12 months) | TikTok | 565,000 |
| Aug 2024 | TikTok | 874,800 |
| Aug 2024 | Instagram | 287,000 |
| 2025 | TikTok | ~871,900 (near 1M) |

**Time elapsed to 100K:** 3 months  
**Time elapsed to 500K+:** ~12 months

### Posting Cadence

- Started: 2 videos per week (TikTok)
- Scaled to: **Daily** (one post per day, every day; followers DM her when she misses a day)
- Production model: One **4-hour monthly batch shoot** with producer Denver Bailey produces **30–50 videos** per session, then released daily
- Newsletter: Weekly (once per week, dictated via phone call with writer, ~1 hour)

### Content Pillar Breakdown

Three stated buckets — every piece of content falls into one:

1. **What would you tell your best friend?** (personal real estate stories, honest market takes)
2. **What would you tell your best client?** (transactional tips, scripts, practical advice)
3. **What would you tell your therapist?** (emotional journey, failures, real life of a broker)

Format: Interview style — Glennda speaks directly to producer Denver Bailey (off-camera), who plays the role of friend/client/therapist. NOT a talking-head selfie video. NOT fancy listing tours. Human storytelling.

### Hook Formula

Every video ends with the same closer:

> "My name is Glennda Baker. I'm a real estate agent in Atlanta, Georgia. Send me a text, tag me in a post, give me a call."

The hook into each video: a real story or hard truth from 30 years of real estate. Example: "I sold 27 houses on Match.com." The viewer watches because the story is interesting; the CTA at the end converts.

Stated philosophy: **"It is not about vanity and virality — it is about value and visibility."**

### Biggest Early Mistake

Two failed starts before finding her formula:
- **2016:** Hired a producer at $450/video for 6 YouTube videos. Total views to date: 2,985. Failed because she lacked a clear voice and the algorithm wasn't ready.
- **2019:** "Posts from the Porsche" — smartphone videos in her parked car. 11 episodes, 1,868 total views. Closer, but no distribution channel.
- **2020:** Found TikTok + found the right collaborator (Denver Bailey) + had clarity of voice after 4 years of consuming content. Then it worked.

**The lesson:** Content format matters less than finding the right platform-collaborator-voice combination. She didn't succeed until all three aligned.

### Tools and Team

- **Producer:** Denver Bailey (Atlanta-based), $5,000–$7,500/month
- **Before content:** Was paying Zillow a similar monthly amount, got 1/3 the business
- **Idea capture:** Google Form pinned as an app on her iPhone. Captures concept + what inspired it + caption draft. Opens the spreadsheet on a laptop at shoot day to run through ideas.
- **Newsletter writer:** Jen (unnamed) — Glennda dictates on a weekly call, Jen writes it
- **No video editor beyond Denver.** She is not producing this herself.

### Estimated Weekly Time Investment

~4–6 hours for content (one monthly 4-hour shoot session ÷ 4 weeks) plus 1 hour for newsletter dictation. The **total creative time investment is approximately 2–3 hours per week** once the batch system is running. The rest is real estate.

### Documented Business Impact

- **2021 GCI from TikTok directly:** $241,000 gross commission income
- **Consistent 30% of real estate business** from social (per her own statements, sustained for 3+ years)
- **2024 production:** $70 million + 75 sides
- **Avg views:** 350,000 per day / 10.8 million per month across platforms
- **Compared to Zillow:** Social media drives 3x more business per dollar spent vs. Zillow ads at the same monthly cost

### Sources

## 2. Kina DeSantis — Irvine, CA

**Name:** Kina DeSantis  
**Market:** Irvine, CA (Orange County)  
**Brokerage:** Reframe Real Estate (boutique, powered by Side)  
**Niche:** First-time buyers, move-up buyers

### Follower Trajectory

| Platform | Followers (2025) |
|----------|-----------------|
| TikTok (@socal_realtor) | 27,400 |
| Instagram | N/A publicly documented |

**Key insight:** She built a $100M+ brokerage with **fewer than 30,000 social followers.** Conversion rate, not follower count, is what she optimized.

### Follower → Revenue Trajectory

| Year | Individual/Brokerage Volume |
|------|---------------------------|
| 2020 (Year 1) | $11 million individual |
| 2021 (Year 2) | $36 million individual |
| 2022 (Year 3) | $40 million brokerage |
| 2023 (Year 4) | $89 million brokerage |
| 2025 (reported) | $102 million brokerage |

**Started as a brand new agent in 2020. Zero prior real estate network. No cold calling, no door knocking — 100% TikTok/Instagram.**

### Posting Cadence

- Daily TikTok and Instagram videos
- Strategy: Answer one real question per video
- Iteration cadence: 1% improvement per video. Not trying to go viral. Trying to be slightly more helpful than the last one.

### Content Pillar Breakdown

Single pillar: **educational real estate content for first-time and move-up buyers**

- Hyper-local Orange County market topics
- Common buyer questions answered plainly ("Why is there a telephone pole in my backyard?")
- No lifestyle content, no luxury aspiration, no personal drama
- She was the first agent in her market doing daily house tours — then everyone copied her

### Hook Formula

Answer a question her clients actually asked her that week. The hook IS the question:

> "Why is there a telephone pole in my backyard?"

Simple, specific, local. When prospects call her, they say "I saw your video about telephone poles." That's the conversion.

### Biggest Early Mistake

Didn't hire help soon enough. She was running every function solo until burnout forced her hand.

> "I was never on a team. It's literally always been me. I just started hiring right away. I hired my cousin to help me edit."

Her first assistant, Patrick, became an agent — because he learned her systems from the inside. **Lesson: hire before you need it.**

### Tools and Team

- Started: Smartphone, solo production
- First hire: Cousin to edit videos
- Second hire: Assistant for showing scheduling
- Coaches: Anthony Marguleas and Zaid Hannah (PartnerUp Coaching / Side)
- CRM: Follow Up Boss (her highest-ROI software by her own statement)

### Estimated Weekly Time Investment

By her own account: **"I worked every moment that I wasn't asleep" for all of 2020, 2021, and 2022.** This was a full-commitment 60–80+ hour week, but most of that was real estate (showings, negotiations), not content. Content was 30–60 minutes daily.

### Documented Business Impact

- TikTok leads convert 3–10x better than Zillow leads (per Knwn Local interview, April 2026)
- **40% of her leads sign a buyer broker agreement** (industry average is much lower)
- $102 million brokerage volume sustained from an account with under 30K followers
- Leads arrive already knowing her wedding, her dogs, her personality — no cold relationship

### Why She Is the Most Actionable Case Study for Small Brokers

- Started with zero sphere in real estate (had technology background, not real estate)
- No famous brokerage brand to lean on initially
- Non-coastal-elite market (Irvine is suburban, not NYC/Beverly Hills)
- Built on **local specificity**, not viral ambition
- Her explicit rule: **"A lot of people go on social media and try to be famous. The key is to be locally famous."**

## 7. Dan Parker — San Diego, CA (YouTube long-form)

**Name:** Dan Parker (@danpbestlifesd)
**Market:** San Diego, CA
**Channel:** YouTube long-form (the only one of these case studies built primarily on long-form, not short-form)

### Why He Is in the Set

Dan Parker is the documented counterexample to the "you have to post daily" rule. He is a San Diego agent who built a successful YouTube channel **posting roughly twice a month for 4+ years.** His channel was highlighted by Jimmy Burgess (Chief Coaching Officer at Real Brokerage, Berkshire Hathaway HomeServices coach) at BAM Fest 2025 as a model for what hyperlocal long-form looks like when it works.

His top-performing videos are not viral entertainment — they are searchable, intent-driven, evergreen guides:

- "Avoid Buying a Home in These San Diego Neighborhoods" (data-driven, negative-framed, drives qualified clicks)
- "A Local's Guide to Oceanside"
- "A Local's Guide to Coronado Island"

### Posting Cadence

- **~2 videos per month** for 4+ years
- Quality and intent over frequency
- Evergreen format — videos keep generating leads months and years after publication

### Format and Production System (per Jimmy Burgess's BAM Fest breakdown)

1. **Title is keyword research first, creative second** — pull what people are actually searching, build the title around it
2. **Script is keyword-optimized** — Burgess explicitly recommends AI assistance (ChatGPT) to draft an SEO-aligned script, then polish in your own voice
3. **B-roll is shot to the script** — videographer captures visuals that match what the agent is saying, not generic stock
4. **Description repeats the script verbatim** — "YouTube reads the description and listens to the video. When everything matches, it increases the clarity and the performance"
5. **Companion landing page per video** — pair each long-form video with a hyperlocal lead-capture URL (Burgess's example: `LiveOakSubdivisionHomesForSale.com`)

### Hook Formula

Three documented formats that work for hyperlocal YouTube:

| Format | Example | Why it works |
|--------|---------|--------------|
| Data-driven negative | "Avoid Buying a Home in These [Area] Neighborhoods" | Strong click intent; positions agent as honest, not salesy; backed by real numbers |
| Local guide | "A Local's Guide to [Neighborhood]" | Pulls relocation searches from feeder markets; ranks in Google + YouTube SEO |
| Compare-and-contrast | "[Your City] vs. [Other City]: Where Should You Live?" | Captures buyers in research phase; "feeder market" content that pulls inbound relocation leads |

### Documented Business Impact

- 4+ years of consistent lead generation from a low-volume channel
- The channel is featured by a national coach (Jimmy Burgess) as a case study, which is itself evidence of trackable results
- Specific GCI not published in source — but Burgess's framing ("posting two high-quality, intentional videos per month can generate massive ROI") is from someone with full visibility into Parker's performance via the Real Brokerage coaching relationship

### Why He Is Relevant for Ryan Realty

Bend → Portland, Bend → Seattle, Bend → San Francisco, Bend → Phoenix, Bend → Denver are all classic feeder-market relocation flows. A Dan Parker–style YouTube channel running compare-and-contrast videos ("Bend vs. Boise: Where Should You Move?") and local guides ("A Local's Guide to NorthWest Crossing") would compound for years. Unlike TikTok/IG (which need daily feeding), YouTube videos sit on Google search and keep working — they are evergreen assets.

**This is the channel that should run in parallel to the Kina DeSantis short-form strategy, not instead of it.** Short-form for top-of-funnel discovery + brand familiarity; long-form for relocation buyers in research mode.

## The 5 Patterns Every Single One of Them Executed

These appear in every case study reviewed, across all markets and follower sizes:

### 1. Consistency before quality

All of them published on a schedule they maintained regardless of engagement. Glennda posted daily even when nobody was watching. Kina posted daily from day one. Daniel Heider created a video for every listing from day one, 15 years ago, before YouTube existed as a real platform. Madison Sutton published through the early months when video was "frowned upon" in NYC real estate.

**The pattern:** They showed up on a schedule. The algorithm rewards consistent posting. Consistency compounds.

### 2. They answered questions people actually had

None of them tried to invent interesting topics. Glennda talked about things that happened in her week. Kina answered questions her buyers asked her that day. Madison Sutton answered NYC apartment hunting questions during COVID. Cash Jordan showed people what NYC apartments actually look like inside.

**The pattern:** Real questions beat manufactured topics every time. They were drawing content from their active real estate practice, not from content calendars.

### 3. They found one platform first and owned it

Glennda: TikTok. Kina: TikTok. Levi Pinson: TikTok. Cash Jordan: YouTube. Madison Sutton: TikTok. Daniel Heider: built the foundation on YouTube-style property video, then ported to TikTok.

None of them tried to be everywhere from day one. They picked the platform where their content format fit the algorithm and focused there. Instagram reposts and cross-posting came later, after the primary channel was proven.

### 4. They were patient about follower count and impatient about habit

Kina DeSantis built a $102M brokerage with 27,400 followers. Her explicit rule: don't measure vanity metrics. Measure whether you posted today, whether you improved slightly, whether your leads are converting. Glennda kept posting when her first six YouTube videos got fewer than 500 views each.

**The pattern:** All of them defined success as "did I post?" not "did I get views?" for the first 6–12 months. The compounding happens invisibly, then suddenly.

### 5. They kept their CTA identical and repeated it on every piece of content

Glennda: "My name is Glennda Baker. I'm a real estate agent in Atlanta, Georgia. Send me a text, tag me in a post, give me a call." — the same 30-word close on every video for 4+ years.

Kina: "Book a call" link in bio, same educational CTA format repeated on every video.

Madison Sutton: "Send me a DM if you want to see this unit." Same invite on every NYC apartment tour.

**The pattern:** Repetition builds recognition. Viewers who see the same consistent CTA across 50 videos know exactly what to do when they're ready to act.

## What the Top Performers Did That the Middling Ones Did Not

Based on the full research set, the top performers — Glennda Baker ($70M+ at a consistent 30% of leads from social), Kina DeSantis ($102M from 27K followers), Daniel Heider (3.7M followers, premier DC market position) — differ from average social-media-active agents in six ways:

### 1. They hired production help before they thought they needed it

Glennda hired Denver Bailey at $5K–$7.5K/month. Kina hired her cousin to edit. Daniel Heider built a production team. None of them were trying to do solo phone selfie videos as a permanent strategy. They identified production as a constraint and removed it early.

Middling performers: continue doing poor-quality phone selfie content forever, then cite "I don't have time" as the reason they stopped.
