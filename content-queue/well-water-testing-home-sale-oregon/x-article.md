Short version

If you are selling a house in Oregon that runs on a private well, a water test is part of the deal. It is not a negotiating chip and it is not something a friendly buyer can wave away. Here is how the process works, in plain terms.

When the duty starts

The seller's responsibility begins once an offer is accepted. At that point the well needs to be tested for three things: arsenic, nitrates, and coliform bacteria. The samples go to an accredited lab. After the reports come back, the seller sends them to the buyer and to the Oregon Health Authority, and the state's data sheet gets filed too.

The results stay valid for a year, which matters if a deal falls apart and you relist. A recent report may still be usable.

Which wells are exempt

Not every water source triggers the rule. According to the Oregon Health Authority, spring wells, wells used only for irrigation, and wells on undeveloped land are exempt. If you are not sure which category your property falls into, ask your broker before you assume.

The buyer cannot waive it

This surprises people. Even if a buyer says they are comfortable skipping the test, the required lab work still has to happen. The rule exists to protect the next owner and the public record, so a handshake agreement does not change it.

The Private Well Addendum

In Oregon, the standard residential sale agreement has the buyer attach a Private Well Addendum. That form sets the schedule. The seller orders the required tests, pays for them, and does it within the number of business days written into the addendum. If that space is left empty, the default is five business days. The seller then passes the reports along promptly as they arrive.

Buyers can ask for more

The state tests are a floor, not a ceiling. Through the same addendum, a buyer can add tests for things like well flow, extra arsenic, nitrate, or bacteria checks, lead, or other water quality concerns. For each added test, the parties mark who pays.

In Central Oregon, where many homes outside town depend on a well, flow can matter as much as chemistry. A well that tests clean but runs slowly is still a problem worth understanding before closing.

What happens if the results are bad

If the reports show substantial problems, the buyer sends the seller a written list. Then both sides get a short window to negotiate. That could mean repairs, treatment equipment, a price change, or something else you work out together. If nobody can agree, the buyer can back out and get the deposits refunded.

This is also where good advice helps. Ask your broker what to request, and talk with a well professional or the lab about what the numbers mean. A result that looks scary on paper is sometimes a straightforward fix, and sometimes it is not.

A practical note for sellers

Do not treat the test as a surprise to be handled after the offer. If you know your well's history, you can answer questions early, set realistic expectations, and avoid a stalled negotiation. If you are getting ready to list, a conversation about the well belongs on your prep list alongside the other inspection items.

A practical note for buyers

Read the addendum closely. Decide which extra tests make sense for the property, and be clear about who pays. A well is part of the house's mechanical system, and it deserves the same attention as the roof or the furnace.
