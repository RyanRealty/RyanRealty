Start before you walk in

New construction is a big part of the market in Bend and Redmond, and most of those sales begin in a builder's model home. The friendly rep greeting you is paid by the builder. That is not a criticism, just the way the arrangement works. If you want someone on your side, sign a buyer agreement with your own broker before the first visit. Some builders have you register on arrival and then refuse to let an agent join later.

Know what kind of home you are buying

A spec home is already built or underway and ready for a buyer. A production home is a builder's floor plan on a builder's lot with a menu of finishes. A semi-custom home begins with a plan you can alter. A custom home is designed around your own lot. In Bend and Redmond, most new homes on the market are production or spec homes in active subdivisions, mainly on the east side of Bend and around Redmond.

The contract is the builder's paper

Expect the builder's own form rather than the standard Oregon purchase agreement. Read the earnest money language closely, especially when the deposit stops being refundable. Check the right to swap materials, the completion date and what follows if it slips, and the scope and length of the warranty.

Upgrades are where the price moves

Model homes are usually shown with the upgraded package, while the advertised price covers the base package. Price each upgrade you want against what similar finishes cost in the resale market. When it is time to negotiate, builders tend to protect the base price because it sets the comps for the neighborhood. They are more flexible on upgrade credits, closing cost help, and rate buydowns through their lender.

Inspect during construction, not just at the end

City and county inspectors check each code stage, but they are checking code, not the quality of the work. Hire your own inspector to look at the framing, plumbing, and electrical before the drywall goes up, and again before your final walk-through. Builders in Central Oregon are used to it. Bring both lists to the walk-through and get every repair committed in writing before you close.

Watch the calendar and your rate lock

Ask for a realistic finish date and build your financing around it. A rate lock has an expiration, and a delayed build can mean paying for an extension or taking a new rate. Builders with their own lender often dangle a long lock or a buydown. Get a quote from an outside lender too, because the incentive can be worth less than the difference in rate.

Warranty, HOA, and Oregon rules

Oregon does not dictate the terms of a new-home warranty. State law does require the contractor to give you a written warranty offer covering defects in materials and workmanship, and you can accept or decline it. The terms belong to the builder, so read them before you sign. The builder must also be licensed with the Construction Contractors Board, which runs a notice-of-defect process for claims. Nearly every new subdivision here has an HOA, so review the dues, rules, and transfer fees before you write an offer.

What the city charges

Cities collect one-time system development charges at permit to cover the water, sewer, and road capacity a new home uses. Bend's published schedule lists them by category, and the parks charge is set separately by the park district, so there is no single all-in number. A builder's price already includes these fees, while a custom build pays them at permit. Newer homes in the region are also built to Oregon's wildfire code, which shapes vents, siding, decking, and the zone around the foundation.

Don't skip the resale comps

Until the lender underwrites the loan, a new home is priced by the builder, not an appraiser. If your price with upgrades sits above what neighbors' homes have closed for, the appraisal can come in low. Before you sign, have your broker pull closed sales from the subdivision and nearby resale homes, so the number you agree to is one the lender will support.
