A home with a septic tank adds a few steps to the sale. Nothing about them is hard, but they catch people off guard when nobody talks them through early. Here is how it works in Oregon.

Where the decisions live

Pumping and inspection are not handled by some automatic rule. They are handled in the paperwork. When a property has a septic or other onsite sewage system, the buyer attaches a septic addendum (OREF 081) to the standard Oregon sale agreement.

The addendum is a checklist. The parties mark which services will happen: pumping or cleaning, locating or digging to reach the system, inspections or tests, or something else. Then, for every service they mark, they choose whether the seller or the buyer pays.

The key point is that the form arrives without a payer selected. That choice is a negotiation, which makes it worth discussing with your broker before you write or accept an offer.

Who orders and who inspects

Whoever is paying for a service is the one who orders it. The addendum sets a deadline in business days, and if nobody fills it in, the default is three. Inspections must be performed by a DEQ-certified professional, so a handy neighbor with a flashlight will not count.

What the seller hands over

The seller shares the system records that exist. Think recent inspections, maintenance contracts, permits and as-built drawings. The same business-day deadline applies. Separately, Oregon's seller property disclosure asks whether and when the tank has been pumped, so that question gets answered on paper too.

When the reports are not clean

If the reports show substantial deficiencies, the buyer sends the seller a list. Then a short negotiating window opens, where both sides try to agree on who repairs what and who covers the cost.

If they cannot agree, the buyer may end the deal and get deposits back. If the buyer lets the window pass without doing that, the buyer is treated as accepting the system as it stands. That last part surprises people, so watch the calendar.

A useful extra option

Anyone can request an evaluation report on an existing system from a qualified evaluator under Oregon DEQ rules. You do not need to wait for the other side to suggest it.

Maintenance timing

DEQ advises having a tank inspected regularly, roughly every 5 to 7 years, and pumped by a DEQ-licensed pumper once solids build past 40 percent. It also recommends hiring a qualified inspector before buying a property with an existing system, which is good advice even when the contract does not require it.

Local rules

Requirements can vary by location, so for anything Deschutes County specific, check with Deschutes County Environmental Soils.

The short version

For buyers: decide early which services you want and who pays, and keep the negotiation window in mind. For sellers: gather your records now, and expect the pumping question to come up. For both: the addendum rewards a conversation before the offer, not after.
